If you build medical devices in Rust, you have probably met a testing problem you have learned to work around: your device is **stochastic**. The same specimen, measured twice, need not return the same result — analytical noise, invalid or QC results, reagent-lot effects, a firmware update, or timing variability all move the outcome. Conventional pass/fail testing assumes a deterministic system and quietly breaks on a stochastic one. The questions that actually matter then get answered by hand, in spreadsheets, or not at all: *Does the device still perform as well as the version we validated? Can we demonstrate that — with confidence intervals, for both functional and temporal behaviour? Will we notice when a reagent lot or a firmware release degrades it?*

[`feotest`](https://github.com/mavai-org/feotest) is an open-source, Rust-native framework built for exactly these questions — and if you have not heard of it, that is expected: the approach itself is not yet widely known. feotest treats a device as a **stochastic service** and turns its variability into a repeatable `measure → verify` loop: first establish a statistically defined baseline, then continuously test whether the current device still conforms to it. Functional outcomes are modelled with Bernoulli/binomial reasoning and reported with confidence intervals (Wilson construction); latency is treated as a distribution rather than an average; and an overall verdict is a conjunction of contract criteria rather than a single deterministic assertion. The underlying methodology is documented at [`r.mavai.org`](https://r.mavai.org/), whose Statistical Companion distinguishes functional stochasticity from temporal stochasticity and pins down every formula the framework ships.

That methodology is not merely documented — feotest is continuously checked against it. The same formulae are independently implemented in R, in [`mavai-R`](https://github.com/mavai-org/mavai-R), the family's statistical oracle, computed against established statistical packages. Each release publishes the results as a suite of reference cases, and feotest carries a conformance test that loads them and asserts its own output agrees to a tolerance of 1e-6 — roughly one part in a million. So feotest does not ask you to take its statistics on trust: every confidence interval, threshold, and latency bound it produces is verified, release by release, against an independent reference implementation of the published methodology. For a tool whose entire purpose is to generate evidence, that is the evidence that the tool itself is correct — the substance behind any software-tool-validation expectation under IEC 62304.

To make this concrete, feotest ships a runnable [medical-device example](https://github.com/mavai-org/feotest-showcase-medical). It is a demonstration of the framework — not a medical-device product, and not a compliance shortcut. It models a diagnostic device whose results vary from specimen to specimen, then characterises a baseline over a reference panel, records covariates such as software version and reagent lot, verifies later runs against the committed baseline, and raises a covariate mismatch when the comparator is no longer valid. That maps directly onto real lifecycle events: firmware releases, assay or reagent-lot changes, instrument drift, and post-market performance monitoring.

The timing is no accident. In the United States, the FDA’s Quality Management System Regulation became effective on 2 February 2026 and incorporates ISO 13485:2016 into the U.S. device quality-system framework, with FDA inspections now aligned to the new QMSR process. For software-heavy devices, FDA guidance also continues to emphasise documented evidence for device software functions, AI-enabled device lifecycle management, predetermined change-control plans, and cybersecurity risk management in premarket submissions.

Each of those maps onto something feotest produces. The `measure → verify` loop is documented design verification for a software device function — objective evidence rather than an anecdote. A FAIL verdict carrying a stated minimum detectable degradation is nonconforming-product detection with quantified confidence, not a judgement call. The distributional, confidence-interval treatment of functional *and* temporal performance is what AI-enabled lifecycle management needs when a single deterministic assertion is meaningless. And the tightest fit is change control: because covariates such as software version and reagent lot are part of baseline identity, a change invalidates the comparator and triggers re-verification automatically — exactly the trigger a predetermined change-control plan is built around. The one premarket emphasis feotest does not touch is cybersecurity, which sits outside its scope.

The European context points the same way. EUDAMED, the European Database on Medical Devices, has moved from long-running rollout to mandatory operational relevance: as of 28 May 2026, the Actor registration, UDI/Device registration, Notified Bodies & Certificates, and Market Surveillance modules are mandatory, while Clinical Investigations/performance studies and Vigilance/post-market surveillance remain under analysis or development. Recent MDCG guidance also sharpens the evidence environment, including 2025 guidance on IVD performance studies and the interplay between MDR/IVDR and the EU AI Act for medical-device AI.

What feotest produces is the evidence EUDAMED's surveillance side consumes. The continuous verify loop *is* post-market performance monitoring; its covariate-mismatch and FAIL signals are the trended functional and temporal data that feed the Market Surveillance module and the forthcoming vigilance and post-market modules. The confidence-interval performance figures speak to the substance MDCG's 2025 IVD performance-study guidance expects, and the distributional treatment suits the AI-enabled performance claims that MDR/IVDR and the EU AI Act jointly scrutinise.

The Rust angle is what makes this immediately usable rather than aspirational. Rust is moving into the safety-critical conversation rather than remaining a general-purpose systems language: the Rust Foundation created the Safety-Critical Rust Consortium in June 2024 to support responsible Rust use in safety-critical software, and the Ferrocene toolchain is positioned as a qualified Rust toolchain for safety- and mission-critical systems, including IEC 62304 Class C medical-device software development. For manufacturers already shipping Rust in device software, a Rust-native probabilistic testing framework is not just convenient; it keeps the evidence-generation layer in the same language as the implementation, the same test harness, the same CI system, and the same release process.

For manufacturers, the offer is practical: define the claim, encode the contract, measure the baseline, version the evidence, and re-run verification whenever the device or its operating context changes. feotest does not replace clinical evaluation, analytical validation, risk management, usability engineering, cybersecurity engineering, or QMS procedures. It does, however, provide a disciplined way to produce durable verification artifacts: baselines, verdicts, confidence floors, latency percentiles, covariate identities, and auditable reports.

For auditors, the value is complementary. A conventional spreadsheet of pass/fail results often hides the assumptions behind sampling, thresholds, confidence, and environmental comparability. feotest exposes those assumptions. An auditor can ask: What claim is being tested? What is the reference population? What confidence level is used? What is the minimum detectable degradation? Which covariates define baseline identity? What happens when the reagent lot changes? Those questions map cleanly onto the methodology and the generated evidence artifacts.

The conclusion is modest but, for the right team, consequential: feotest is not a regulatory solution, but it is a credible, open-source way to build an **executable evidence loop** for stochastic medical-device behaviour — and the medical-device example shows that loop end to end. In a regulatory environment increasingly concerned with lifecycle control, software change, AI-enabled functionality, post-market monitoring, traceability, and centralised registration infrastructure, that is relevant to manufacturers and auditors alike. The promise is not that feotest proves a device is safe or effective; it is that feotest lets you continuously verify that a previously validated device still behaves like the device it claims to be — in the language your device is already written in.
